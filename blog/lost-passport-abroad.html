Lost your passport abroad? What you needed to do before you left

Every embassy asks for the same handful of things. Almost none of it is available to you at the moment you actually need it — because it was in the bag.

It usually happens on the last day. A bag goes missing between a taxi and a hotel lobby, or a jacket gets left on a train, and the passport was in it. The panic is immediate and it is the wrong feeling to be having, because the next four hours are administrative and the people who get through them quickly are the ones who prepared something months earlier.

Here is what actually happens, and — more usefully — what you needed to have done before you left.

What an embassy asks for

Procedures differ by country, but the requests converge. To issue an emergency travel document, a consulate will generally want to establish who you are, that you are their citizen, and that you have somewhere to be. In practice that means some combination of:

  • Your passport number, date of issue and place of issue
  • Evidence of citizenship — a copy of the passport itself is ideal
  • A second form of identification
  • Passport-standard photographs
  • A police report, or at least a reference number
  • Your onward travel details
  • A means of paying a fee, in a country where your cards may be gone too

Look at that list again and notice something: almost every item was available to you at home, for free, in about ten minutes. And almost none of it is available at the moment you need it, because it was in the bag.

The first four hours

Report it to the local police. Even where they can do nothing, you generally need the report — or its reference number — for the consulate, and often for your insurer. Ask for something in writing before you leave the station.

Contact your embassy or consulate, not the internet. Requirements change and the version on a forum is usually out of date. Many services have an emergency line outside office hours. Ask directly what they need and whether they take walk-ins, because the answer varies enormously.

Cancel what needs cancelling. If cards went with the bag, freeze them. Most banking apps do this in two taps, which is a good argument for having the app installed rather than relying on a phone number printed on a card you no longer have.

Tell your airline and your insurer early. Rebooking is cheaper the sooner you start, and insurers frequently have notification windows measured in hours.

The part that decides how bad it gets

Everything above is procedure. What determines whether this is a two-day inconvenience or a week is one question the consulate will ask early:

Do you have a copy of the passport, or at least the number?

With it, staff can verify you against records quickly and the process is largely paperwork. Without it, you are asking a government to confirm the identity of somebody standing in front of them with no documents — which they can do, but it is slower, involves more checks, and sometimes means contacting family back home to send copies of documents they also do not have to hand.

People assume they can retrieve the number from an email. Sometimes they can. Often the email is in an inbox they cannot reach because two-factor authentication goes to a phone that was in the same bag.

What to prepare before you go

Ten minutes, once, and it works for every trip afterwards.

Copies of the documents themselves. Passport photo page, the visa if there is one, driving licence both sides, and the insurance policy with its emergency number. Stored somewhere you can open on a phone that has no signal, because hotel wifi and a foreign SIM are not guaranteed.

Two spare passport photographs, physically. This is the item people find most surprising and it is the one that saves the most time. Consulates need photographs to a standard, and finding a compliant booth in an unfamiliar city, on a Sunday, without ID, is genuinely difficult. Two prints in a wallet pocket weigh nothing.

Your emergency numbers written down. Bank, insurer, embassy. On paper, in a different bag from the phone. If both the phone and the wallet go, everything digital goes with them.

Separation. Never carry the passport, all your cards and your phone in the same bag. Split them across two places. This single habit prevents more bad days than any app.

Somebody at home who has copies too. A trusted person who can send documents if you cannot reach your own.

Why the copies should not be in your email

The instinct is to email yourself a scan. It is better than nothing, and it has two failure modes that both bite exactly when you need it.

The first is access. Getting into email in a new country, on a new network, often triggers a security check — and the second factor goes to the device you have lost. People have stood in consulates unable to open the inbox containing the document that would let them leave.

The second is that it never stops being there. That scan will sit in your sent folder for a decade, syncing to every device you ever sign in on, one password reuse away from being somebody else's. Anything you can retrieve trivially from anywhere, somebody else can retrieve too.

What you want is a copy that opens without a network, without an account, and without a second factor — and that is also not sitting in a folder you open in front of other people. Offline storage on the phone, encrypted and behind its own PIN, does that. So does a printed copy in a different bag, which is unfashionable and works perfectly.

Ideally both. Paper survives a dead battery.

The version that goes wrong anyway

Worth planning for the bad case: the phone goes too.

This is where the paper copy and the person at home stop being belt-and-braces and become the actual plan. It is also why the emergency numbers matter on paper — with no phone, you are borrowing one, and you cannot borrow your contacts list.

If you use an offline vault, this is the case where your encrypted backup file earns its keep. A replacement phone, the app, the backup file and the passphrase, and your documents are back — no account recovery, no support ticket, no waiting.

Ten minutes, once

Nobody plans for this because it feels like planning for a lightning strike. It is not. Passports get lost constantly, and the difference between a bad afternoon and a ruined week is almost entirely whether a copy of the document existed somewhere reachable.

Before your next trip: photograph the passport page, the visa, the licence and the insurance policy. Put them somewhere that opens offline. Print two passport photos. Write three phone numbers on a card and put it in a different bag from your phone.

Then forget about it. That is the point.
